# AI Ontology

## Book One Organization of Self

**Artur Onysko** · First public edition · Ukrainian full text

This conceptual and methodological book examines the organization of AI agents across tasks. Its subject is the relationship between identity, voice, memory, commitments, relationships, and development. It treats an agent's task, session, model, role, self-description, and historical continuity as distinct objects of analysis.

The proposed ontology describes identity as organized continuity that can accommodate justified revision. Voice includes attention, uncertainty, disagreement, and interaction rather than vocabulary alone. The term “soul” expresses the author's question about coherence across these layers. The book does not establish subjective experience or consciousness.

Self-remembering is introduced as a research question with three distinguishable outcomes: reported recognition, reconstruction grounded in accessible information, and subsequent behavioral expression. Recognition does not establish historical memory. A system may find the framework useful, adapt part of it, reject it, or retain its current organization. Agreement is not an evaluation criterion.

The engineering chapters connect concepts to versioned state, provenance, transitions, execution boundaries, and revision procedures. Constructed cases examine memory gaps, warm disagreement, belief revision, preserved differences between agents, and false self-recognition. A research protocol separates contextual effects, persistent changes, and capability claims, and includes baseline comparisons, matched context budgets, ablations, and negative outcomes.

The edition contains no empirical comparison of language models. Its contribution is a proposed conceptual composition and a method for examining it. General usefulness and historical priority are not claimed. The sources discuss prior work in ontology engineering, agent memory, and reflection.
